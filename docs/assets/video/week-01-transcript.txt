WHY LOGICAL FORM MATTERS

Welcome to CS104 and Logical Reasoning, Part One. Logic gives us a precise way to answer a deceptively simple question: does a conclusion really follow from the information before it? In ordinary conversation, a claim can feel persuasive because of its topic, tone, or familiarity. Formal logic asks us to temporarily set those features aside and expose the underlying structure. We will begin with statements and logical operators, use truth tables to compare compound forms, study conditional statements and their related forms, and finish with standard rules of inference and argument validity. As you watch, pause before worked answers and make a prediction. The goal is not to memorise symbols in isolation. It is to develop a reliable habit: identify the form, examine the relevant truth conditions, and justify the conclusion.

STATEMENTS AND SYMBOLIC FORM

Our first object is a statement, sometimes called a proposition. A statement is a declarative sentence that is true or false, but not both. Four is an integer is a statement, and it is true. Paris is the capital of England is also a statement, although it is false. A command such as open the door is not a statement, because it does not assert a fact. A question is not a statement either. Be careful with sentences whose truth value is unknown to us. A statement does not stop being a statement merely because we do not know whether it is true. The requirement is that it has a truth value in principle. This distinction lets logic focus on assertions that can participate in truth-preserving reasoning.

STATEMENTS AND SYMBOLIC FORM

We represent statements using variables such as p, q, and r. Suppose p means Jane is a mathematics major, q means Jane is a computer science major, and r means Jane will take CS104. Replacing complete sentences with variables is not an attempt to remove meaning. It allows us to see the reusable pattern underneath the meaning. An argument about Jane, an argument about divisibility, and an argument about program correctness may share exactly the same logical form. Once a form is known to be valid, every correct substitution into that form inherits the same guarantee. Always maintain a small dictionary connecting each variable to its sentence. Most translation mistakes come from silently changing what a variable means or losing track of which expression is a premise and which is the conclusion.

STATEMENTS AND SYMBOLIC FORM

The simplest logical operator is negation. If p is a statement, not p is written with the negation symbol and has the opposite truth value. If p is Paris is the capital of England, then not p is Paris is not the capital of England. When p is true, not p is false, and when p is false, not p is true. In English, negation can appear as not, it is not the case that, no, never, or another phrase. Translate the intended meaning rather than searching mechanically for a single word. Also pay attention to scope. Negating an entire compound statement is different from negating just one component. Parentheses show the scope explicitly, and later De Morgan's laws will tell us how to move a negation through a compound expression without changing its meaning.

STATEMENTS AND SYMBOLIC FORM

A conjunction joins p and q with the word and. It is written p and q, using the wedge symbol. A conjunction is true only when both components are true. This requirement is strict. If either component is false, the entire conjunction is false. Consider Paris is the capital of England and London is the capital of England. The second component is true, but the first is false, so the conjunction is false. In a longer conjunction, every component must be true. This makes conjunction useful for expressing a collection of simultaneous requirements, such as the preconditions of an algorithm or the properties an object must satisfy. When you evaluate a compound form, compute the inner components first and record intermediate values. That simple discipline prevents many errors in longer truth tables.

STATEMENTS AND SYMBOLIC FORM

A disjunction joins p and q with or, written using the vee symbol. In mathematics, or is inclusive unless we explicitly say otherwise. The expression p or q is true when p is true, q is true, or both are true. It is false only when both components are false. Everyday language sometimes uses exclusive or, meaning exactly one of two alternatives, as in soup or salad. Do not automatically import that reading into symbolic logic. If exclusivity matters, it must be represented explicitly. Compare conjunction and disjunction as requirements. And asks for all listed conditions; inclusive or asks for at least one. This difference becomes especially important when we negate compound statements and when we translate program conditions with multiple Boolean tests.

STATEMENTS AND SYMBOLIC FORM

Compound statements can combine several operators, so order matters. Negation is normally performed first. Conjunction and disjunction are evaluated next, and implication later. Conjunction and disjunction have the same precedence in this course, so if both appear without parentheses the expression may be ambiguous. Parentheses are always the safest choice because they make the intended structure visible. Compare not open parenthesis p and q close parenthesis with open parenthesis not p close parenthesis and q. The first negates the entire conjunction; the second negates only p. These forms do not generally have the same truth value. Think of a statement form as a syntax tree: evaluate the deepest grouped operations, then work outward one layer at a time.

TRUTH TABLES AND EQUIVALENCE

A statement form is an expression built from statement variables and logical operators. It becomes an actual statement when concrete statements are substituted for the variables. A truth table examines the form systematically by listing every possible assignment of truth values. With one variable there are two rows. With two variables there are four rows. With three variables there are eight rows. In general, n variables require two to the power n rows. Arrange the rows in a consistent pattern so no assignment is duplicated or omitted. Then create a column for each useful intermediate expression. The table is not busywork: it is a complete proof about the truth behaviour of the form because it checks every possible substitution pattern.

TRUTH TABLES AND EQUIVALENCE

Here the expression is not p and q, and p or q. Work from the inner columns. First compute p and q. Negate that result. Separately compute p or q. Finally combine those two columns with and. The expression is true exactly when at least one of p or q is true, but not both. That is the exclusive-or pattern. Notice how an operation we did not treat as primitive can be constructed from negation, conjunction, and disjunction. This is a common theme in logic and computing: a small set of operators can express many useful conditions. At this point, pause and predict the result when p is true and q is false. The conjunction p and q is false, its negation is true, and p or q is true, so the final expression is true.

TRUTH TABLES AND EQUIVALENCE

Two statement forms are logically equivalent when their truth values match for every possible assignment of their variables. We write an equivalence symbol between the forms. Logical equivalence is stronger than two sentences happening to share a truth value today. It says the forms can never disagree under any substitution. A truth table proves equivalence when the final columns are identical row by row. For example, p or q and q or p always match, so disjunction is commutative. Double negation, not not p, also has the same truth value as p on every row. Equivalent forms may be substituted for one another during a derivation without changing the truth conditions of the larger expression. This is why equivalence laws function like algebraic identities for logic.

TRUTH TABLES AND EQUIVALENCE

De Morgan's laws describe how negation interacts with and and or. Not open parenthesis p and q close parenthesis is equivalent to not p or not q. Not open parenthesis p or q close parenthesis is equivalent to not p and not q. To understand the first law, ask what makes a conjunction fail. It fails when at least one component is false, so its negation says not p or not q. To understand the second, ask what makes a disjunction fail. Inclusive or is false only when both components are false, so its negation says not p and not q. When a negation moves through parentheses, negate every component and switch the operator. Remembering both actions prevents the common error of changing only the variables while leaving the connecting operator unchanged.

TRUTH TABLES AND EQUIVALENCE

A small library of equivalence laws makes simplification efficient. Commutative laws allow the order of p and q to switch under and or or. Associative laws allow regrouping repeated conjunctions or repeated disjunctions. Distributive laws expand one operator across the other, much like multiplication distributes over addition, although in logic both operators distribute over each other. Identity laws say p and true is p, while p or false is p. Domination laws say p or true is true, while p and false is false. These laws are not rules of inference. An equivalence replaces one expression with another expression having exactly the same truth values. Keep that distinction clear when you later work with premises and conclusions.

TRUTH TABLES AND EQUIVALENCE

Other useful laws include idempotence, double negation, De Morgan's laws, and absorption. Idempotence says repeating the same condition changes nothing: p and p is p, and p or p is p. Absorption says p or open parenthesis p and q close parenthesis reduces to p, because when p is true the whole disjunction is already true, and when p is false the conjunction cannot rescue it. Its dual form is p and open parenthesis p or q close parenthesis equivalent to p. Do not apply laws only by visual resemblance. Name the subexpression that plays each role, perform one justified replacement, and preserve parentheses. A clear sequence of small equivalence steps is easier to verify than a large unexplained jump.

TRUTH TABLES AND EQUIVALENCE

To show that two forms are not logically equivalent, one counterexample is enough. Consider not open parenthesis p and q close parenthesis and not p and not q. Let p be true and q be false. The conjunction p and q is false, so its negation is true. But not p is false and not q is true, making their conjunction false. The two forms disagree, so they cannot be equivalent. A complete truth table will display every disagreement, but for non-equivalence the first counterexample settles the question. This suggests an efficient strategy: understand what each form requires and deliberately choose values likely to separate them. Counterexamples are a powerful theme throughout discrete mathematics; they refute universal claims by showing one permitted case where the claim fails.

CONDITIONAL STATEMENTS

We now turn to conditional statements. A conditional has form if p, then q, written p implies q. The statement p is the hypothesis and q is the conclusion. The conditional does not assert that p is true. It says that in every case where p is true, q must also be true. Think of p as triggering a promise about q. If you do not hand in homework, then you will get zero marks. If the hypothesis occurs and the conclusion does not, the promise is broken. If the hypothesis does not occur, that particular promise has not been violated. This reading explains the truth table and helps separate logical implication from causation. A conditional can be logically true even when the two component statements have no causal connection.

CONDITIONAL STATEMENTS

The truth table for p implies q has exactly one false row: p is true and q is false. When both are true, the promise is fulfilled. When p is false, the conditional is true regardless of q; this is sometimes called vacuous truth. It may initially feel unusual, so focus on what the conditional forbids. It forbids a true hypothesis with a false conclusion and says nothing further. Suppose the rule is if a shape is a square, then it has four sides. A triangle does not violate the rule, whether or not some other property is true of it, because the triangle does not satisfy the hypothesis. In proofs, this truth condition allows us to establish a conditional by showing the hypothesis cannot occur, although such arguments should still be explained clearly.

CONDITIONAL STATEMENTS

A conditional is logically equivalent to not p or q. Compare their truth tables: both are false only when p is true and q is false. This equivalence helps us rewrite everyday sentences. You get to work on time or you are fired has form p or q. To express it as an if-then statement, view p or q as not not p or q. It becomes: if you do not get to work on time, then you are fired. The same equivalence gives the correct negation. Negating p implies q means negating not p or q. De Morgan's law produces p and not q. In words, the negation of if p then q is p occurs and q does not. Merely reversing or negating the conditional arrow does not produce its logical negation.

CONDITIONAL STATEMENTS

When implications appear inside larger forms, operator precedence becomes important. Negation is evaluated first. Conjunction and disjunction come next. Implication is performed last unless parentheses override the order. For example, p or not q implies r should be read as open parenthesis p or not q close parenthesis implies r. In formal work, add parentheses even when the conventional order would technically resolve the expression. Precision helps both the writer and the reader. When building a truth table, create columns in evaluation order: not q, then p or not q, then the conditional. This mirrors the syntax of the form and makes it easier to locate an error. An unexplained final column is difficult to audit; well-chosen intermediate columns turn the computation into a visible chain of reasoning.

CONDITIONAL STATEMENTS

Every conditional p implies q has three commonly discussed relatives. The contrapositive is not q implies not p. The converse is q implies p. The inverse is not p implies not q. A conditional is always logically equivalent to its contrapositive. The converse and inverse are equivalent to each other, but neither is generally equivalent to the original. Suppose if Howard can swim across the lake, then Howard can swim to the island. Its contrapositive says if Howard cannot swim to the island, then he cannot swim across the lake. That follows: anyone capable of the longer swim is capable of the shorter one. The converse claims that swimming to the island guarantees swimming across the lake, which may be false. Reversing the arrow changes what is being guaranteed.

CONDITIONAL STATEMENTS

The phrase only if often causes translation errors. P only if q means p implies q. It says p cannot happen without q, so q is necessary for p. A useful test is to paraphrase: if p occurs, then q must occur. By contrast, p if q means q implies p. Here q is enough to guarantee p. Pay attention to the word order instead of assuming the first clause is always the hypothesis. For example, you may enter only if you have a ticket means if you enter, then you have a ticket. It does not say that everyone with a ticket necessarily enters. The ticket is necessary under this sentence, but not declared sufficient. Writing a temporary variable dictionary and drawing the arrow explicitly is the safest translation method.

CONDITIONAL STATEMENTS

A biconditional p if and only if q is written p if-and-only-if q. It is true when p and q have the same truth value and false when their values differ. The biconditional is equivalent to the conjunction of both directions: p implies q and q implies p. Therefore, proving an if-and-only-if statement normally requires two separate arguments. First assume p and establish q. Then assume q and establish p. The abbreviation iff, spelled i f f, is common in mathematical writing. Do not treat it as decorative emphasis. It states both necessity and sufficiency. A program is correct if and only if it produces correct answers for every allowed input means correctness guarantees the output condition, and the output condition also guarantees correctness under the definition being used.

CONDITIONAL STATEMENTS

Necessary and sufficient conditions describe the two sides of an implication. In p implies q, p is sufficient for q because p guarantees q. The statement q is necessary for p because p cannot occur without q. For the voting example, if John is eligible to vote then John is at least eighteen. Eligibility is sufficient for the age conclusion, while being at least eighteen is necessary for eligibility. The sentence does not say that age alone is sufficient; other eligibility requirements may exist. A condition is both necessary and sufficient exactly when the biconditional holds. When reading a theorem, label the hypothesis as a sufficient condition for the conclusion and the conclusion as a necessary condition for the hypothesis. This gives you a reliable way to unpack unfamiliar mathematical language.

ARGUMENTS AND VALIDITY

An argument is a sequence of statements ending with a conclusion. The earlier statements are premises. An argument form is valid when no matter which concrete statements replace its variables, if all premises are true, the conclusion is also true. Validity is a structural guarantee. It does not say that the premises in a particular argument are actually true. Consider: if Zeus is human, then Zeus is mortal; Zeus is not mortal; therefore Zeus is not human. This uses a valid form even though the topic is mythical. Conversely, an invalid argument may happen to end with a true conclusion. We evaluate validity by asking whether true premises and a false conclusion are possible together—not by asking whether the conclusion sounds believable.

ARGUMENTS AND VALIDITY

A rule of inference is an argument form already known to be valid. Direct implication, often called modus ponens, has form p implies q, p, therefore q. If three divides the sum of the digits of two hundred fifty-eight, then three divides two hundred fifty-eight. Three does divide the digit sum, so three divides the number. Contrapositive implication, or modus tollens, has form p implies q, not q, therefore not p. If three divided the digit sum of one hundred ten, it would divide the number. Three does not divide one hundred ten, so it does not divide the digit sum. These rules move from premises to a conclusion; they are different from equivalence laws, which replace one expression with another identical in truth value.

ARGUMENTS AND VALIDITY

Specialisation allows us to infer either component from a conjunction: p and q, therefore p. If n divides six and n divides fifteen, then in particular n divides six. Elimination, also called disjunctive syllogism, combines p or q with not p to conclude q. If n is even or m is odd, and n is not even, then m is odd. Notice the exact role of the negated premise. From p or q and p, we may not conclude not q because the disjunction is inclusive; both components could be true. Matching a situation to a rule requires matching every premise, including negations and connective types. State the rule and show the substitution whenever the match might not be obvious.

ARGUMENTS AND VALIDITY

Transitivity chains conditionals. From p implies q and q implies r, conclude p implies r. If eighteen divides n, then nine divides n. If nine divides n, then three divides n. Therefore, if eighteen divides n, three divides n. Division into cases handles a disjunction. From p or q, p implies r, and q implies r, conclude r. If n is positive or n is negative, and either case implies n is nonzero, then n is nonzero. The cases must cover the possibilities given by the disjunction, and each case must reach the same conclusion. These patterns appear constantly in proofs and algorithms. Recognising them frees attention for the mathematical content while preserving a checkable logical skeleton.

ARGUMENTS AND VALIDITY

A truth table provides a general test for validity. Build columns for every premise and for the conclusion. Then identify the critical rows, the rows where all premises are true simultaneously. Only those rows matter to the validity guarantee. If the conclusion is true in every critical row, the argument form is valid. If even one critical row has a false conclusion, that row is a counterexample and the form is invalid. Rows with a false premise cannot violate the definition because validity promises what happens when all premises are true. This method also explains why an argument with inconsistent premises is technically valid: there are no critical rows. In ordinary mathematical writing, however, we prefer informative arguments with premises that can be satisfied.

ARGUMENTS AND VALIDITY

Let us finish with a logic puzzle. Exactly one of four suspects killed Ama, and exactly one suspect is telling the truth. Suspect one says suspect three killed Ama. Suspect two says suspect four did not kill Ama. Suspect three says suspects one and four did not kill Ama. Suspect four says suspect three did not kill Ama. Represent these statements, then test each possible killer. If suspect one is the killer, several statements are true. The same problem occurs for suspect two and suspect three. If suspect four is the killer, suspect one's claim is false, suspect two's claim is false, and suspect three's conjunction is false because suspect four did kill Ama. Suspect four's statement is true because suspect three did not. Exactly one statement is true, so suspect four is the unique solution.

SUMMARY AND NEXT STEPS

We have built a toolkit for precise reasoning. Statements carry truth values and combine through negation, conjunction, disjunction, implication, and biconditional. Truth tables examine every assignment and establish logical equivalence. De Morgan's laws and other identities let us rewrite forms without changing meaning. A conditional is false only when its hypothesis is true and its conclusion false; it is equivalent to not p or q, and its contrapositive is equivalent to the original. Necessary and sufficient conditions describe the direction of the implication. Finally, rules of inference give valid moves from premises to a conclusion, while critical truth-table rows provide a general validity test. Now complete the mastery check.

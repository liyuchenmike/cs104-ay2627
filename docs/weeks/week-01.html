1 · Logical form

Statements have a truth value

A statement, also called a proposition, is a declarative sentence that is either true or false—but not both. “Four is even” is a statement. “Close the window” and “What time is it?” are not statements because they do not assert something that can be assigned a truth value.

Separate form from content

We use letters such as p, q and r as statement variables. This lets us study the structure of reasoning even when the subject changes.

If p means “Jane is a mathematics major,” q means “Jane is a computer science major,” and r means “Jane will take CS104,” then an argument can be represented compactly as p ∨ q, q, ∴ r. The symbols expose its form so we can ask whether the conclusion actually follows.

The basic operators

Disjunctionp ∨ qinclusive “or”; true when at least one is true.
Conditionalp → q“if p, then q”; false only when p is true and q is false.

Worked example: build from the inside out

Evaluate ¬(p ∧ q) ∧ (p ∨ q) when p is true and q is false.

  1. p ∧ q is false because a conjunction needs two true components.
  2. ¬(p ∧ q) is therefore true.
  3. p ∨ q is true because inclusive “or” needs at least one true component.
  4. The outer conjunction combines true with true, so the whole expression is true.

Common mistake: reading ∨ as “one but not both”

Unless explicitly stated otherwise, mathematical disjunction is inclusive. p ∨ q is true when p alone is true, q alone is true, or both are true. Exclusive-or is a different operation.

2 · Same meaning, different form

Logical equivalence compares every possibility

Two statement forms are logically equivalent when they have identical truth values for every possible assignment of their variables. We write P ≡ Q. One matching example is not enough; every row must match.

¬(p ∧ q) ≡ ¬p ∨ ¬q    and    ¬(p ∨ q) ≡ ¬p ∧ ¬q

De Morgan’s laws say that negating a grouped “and” changes it into an “or” of negations, while negating a grouped “or” changes it into an “and” of negations. The operator changes because the condition for the original compound statement to fail changes.

Why De Morgan’s law makes sense

Negate: “John is six feet tall and weighs at least 200 pounds.”

For the original conjunction to be false, at least one component must fail. Its correct negation is: “John is not six feet tall or John does not weigh at least 200 pounds.” Saying “not tall and not heavy” is too strong; it misses cases where only one property fails.

Equivalences worth recognising

Commutativep ∨ q ≡ q ∨ pp ∧ q ≡ q ∧ p
Associative(p ∨ q) ∨ r ≡ p ∨ (q ∨ r)(p ∧ q) ∧ r ≡ p ∧ (q ∧ r)
Distributivep ∨ (q ∧ r) ≡ (p ∨ q) ∧ (p ∨ r)p ∧ (q ∨ r) ≡ (p ∧ q) ∨ (p ∧ r)
Double negation¬¬p ≡ p
Absorptionp ∨ (p ∧ q) ≡ pp ∧ (p ∨ q) ≡ p
Identity / dominationp ∧ T ≡ p; p ∨ F ≡ pp ∨ T ≡ T; p ∧ F ≡ F

How to disprove equivalence efficiently

You do not need a complete truth table if you can find a single counterexample: one assignment on which the two forms differ. For example, ¬(p ∧ q) and ¬p ∧ ¬q differ when p is true and q is false. The first is true; the second is false.

3 · If–then reasoning

A conditional rules out one case

In p → q, p is the hypothesis and q is the conclusion. The conditional promises that whenever p occurs, q follows. It is broken only by a true hypothesis paired with a false conclusion.

p → q ≡ ¬p ∨ q

This equivalence explains why a conditional with a false hypothesis is considered true: ¬p is already true. It does not claim that q is caused by p; logical implication records a truth condition, not necessarily a causal relationship.

Rewriting a real sentence

“You get to work on time or you are fired.” Let p mean “you get to work on time” and q mean “you are fired.” The sentence has form p ∨ q.

Using p → q ≡ ¬p ∨ q, it becomes: “If you do not get to work on time, then you are fired.” Notice that the hypothesis is the negation of p.

Negating an if–then statement

To make p → q false, p must be true and q false. Therefore:

¬(p → q) ≡ p ∧ ¬q

The negation of “If my car is in the repair shop, then I cannot get to class” is “My car is in the repair shop and I can get to class.”

Related conditionals

Originalp → q
Contrapositive¬q → ¬pAlways equivalent to the original.
Converseq → pNot generally equivalent to the original.
Inverse¬p → ¬qEquivalent to the converse, not generally to the original.

Common mistake: reversing the arrow

From “If Howard can swim across the lake, then Howard can swim to the island,” you may infer the contrapositive: “If Howard cannot swim to the island, then Howard cannot swim across the lake.” You may not infer the converse; swimming to the island might be much easier.

Only if, biconditional and conditions

“p only if q” means p → q: q is necessary for p. “p if q” means q → p: q is sufficient for p. “p if and only if q,” written p ↔ q, means both directions hold.

p ↔ q ≡ (p → q) ∧ (q → p)

If “John is eligible to vote” implies “John is at least 18,” then eligibility is a sufficient condition for being at least 18, while being at least 18 is a necessary condition for eligibility. Necessary does not automatically mean sufficient.

4 · Rules of inference

Validity protects the move from premises to conclusion

An argument is a sequence of premises followed by a conclusion. An argument form is valid when there is no substitution of statements that makes all its premises true and its conclusion false. Validity concerns the structure of the inference—not whether the actual premises happen to be true.

Testing validity with critical rows

Consider premises p → (q ∨ ¬r) and q → (p ∧ r), with conclusion ∴ p → r.

  1. Construct columns for the components, both premises and the conclusion.
  2. Identify the critical rows where every premise is true.
  3. Inspect the conclusion only in those critical rows.
  4. If the conclusion is true in every critical row, the argument is valid. One false conclusion in a critical row makes it invalid.

Detective Sam’s four suspects

Exactly one suspect killed Ama and exactly one suspect is telling the truth:

  • Suspect 1: “Suspect 3 killed Ama.”
  • Suspect 2: “Suspect 4 did not kill Ama.”
  • Suspect 3: “Suspects 1 and 4 did not kill Ama.”
  • Suspect 4: “Suspect 3 did not kill Ama.”

Test each possible killer. If Suspect 4 is the killer, statements 1, 2 and 3 are false while statement 4 is true—exactly one truth. Every other candidate produces more than one true statement. Therefore, Suspect 4 killed Ama.

Valid does not mean “conclusion sounds reasonable”

A valid form guarantees only this: if all premises are true, the conclusion cannot be false. An argument may be valid with false premises, and an invalid argument can accidentally have a true conclusion.

Chapter 1 · Why logical form matters

Logical reasoning asks whether a conclusion follows because of an argument’s structure. By replacing content with statement variables, we can evaluate a form once and reuse the result across mathematics, computing and everyday decisions.

Chapter 2 · Statements and operators

A statement is true or false, but not both. Negation reverses a truth value. Conjunction requires both components. Inclusive disjunction requires at least one. Parentheses and the conventional order—negation, conjunction/disjunction, implication—remove ambiguity.

Chapter 3 · Truth tables and equivalence

A truth table examines every assignment. Equivalent forms match on every row. De Morgan’s laws distribute negation while switching and with or. A single row on which two forms differ is enough to disprove equivalence.

Chapter 4 · Conditional statements

The conditional p implies q is false only when p is true and q false. It is equivalent to not p or q. Its negation is p and not q. The contrapositive is equivalent to the original; the converse and inverse are not generally equivalent to it.

Chapter 5 · Biconditionals and conditions

A biconditional requires both implication directions. “p only if q” makes q necessary for p, while “p if q” makes q sufficient for p. Keeping the arrow direction explicit prevents the most common translation errors.

Chapter 6 · Arguments and validity

An argument is valid when no critical row has true premises and a false conclusion. Standard rules—direct implication, contrapositive implication, specialisation, elimination, transitivity and cases—package forms already known to be valid.

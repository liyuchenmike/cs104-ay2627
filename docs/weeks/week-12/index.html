Week 12 · Counting

How can we count possibilities without listing every one?

Choose the right rule for ordered choices, sets and teams, overlapping cases, and selections that allow repetition.

By the end, you can…

  • count inclusive integer lists and multi-step choices;
  • recognise when a simple product fails because choices depend on earlier steps;
  • distinguish permutations from combinations;
  • use addition, complements and inclusion–exclusion without double counting;
  • count repeated objects and unordered selections with repetition.

1 · Count both ends

Inclusive integer lists have one more item than gaps

From 5 through 12, there are seven steps between the endpoints but eight integers: 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11 and 12. In general, integers from m through n, with m ≤ n, number n − m + 1. The plus one includes the starting value. If m = n, the formula correctly gives one item.

Number of integers in [m, n] = n − m + 1   (m ≤ n)

Count multiples by their multipliers

Three-digit multiples of 5 start at 100 = 5 × 20 and end at 995 = 5 × 199. Each eligible multiplier from 20 through 199 corresponds to one eligible number, so the count is 199 − 20 + 1 = 180.

The upper endpoint is 995, although the range of three-digit integers ends at 999. First identify the valid objects, then apply the inclusive-list formula to their labels.

Subtracting endpoints counts gaps

Forgetting the plus one would count the gaps between the first and last integers. Test a proposed formula on a one-item interval to catch this error.

2 · Build an outcome step by step

Multiply choices only when the step counts stay fixed

A four-symbol PIN may use any of 26 letters or 10 digits in each position. Because repetition is allowed, every position has 36 choices, regardless of earlier symbols. The multiplication rule gives 36 × 36 × 36 × 36 = 1,679,616 PINs. Each complete PIN is produced by exactly one sequence of four choices.

Fixed step counts n₁, n₂, …, nₖ ⇒ total n₁ × n₂ × ⋯ × nₖ

Reorder constrained choices

Choose a president, treasurer and secretary from Ann, Bob, Cyd and Dan. Ann cannot be president; Cyd or Dan must be secretary. The tempting count 3 × 3 × 2 = 18 assumes two eligible secretaries survive every earlier choice. They do not.

Choose the secretary first: 2 options. The president then has 2 options, excluding Ann and the secretary. The treasurer has 2 remaining options. The valid count is 2 × 2 × 2 = 8, matching the slide's decision tree.

Constraints can make a product invalid

If a later number of choices varies by branch, a single product of fixed factors is not justified. Split the branches and add them, or choose the most restricted position first.

3 · Order changes the outcome

Permutations fill ordered positions

A permutation is an ordering of distinct objects. With n distinct objects, the first position has n choices, the second n − 1, and so on. Multiplying gives n!. If only r positions are filled without reusing an object, stop after r factors:

P(n, r) = n(n − 1)⋯(n − r + 1) = n!/(n − r)!   (1 ≤ r ≤ n)

Keep “CO” together in COMPUTER

All eight letters are distinct, so unrestricted arrangements number 8! = 40,320. If C and O must remain together in that order, treat the fixed block CO as one object. Arrange it with the six other letters: 7! = 5,040. If OC were also allowed, the answer would be different.

For a partial ordering, P(7, 4) = 7 × 6 × 5 × 4 = 840. There are four ordered positions, not merely a four-element group.

A block can have an internal order

Do not multiply by 2 for the block CO when the question fixes its order. Read whether “together” permits both internal orders.

4 · Combine and correct counts

Add disjoint cases, subtract complements, correct overlaps

If outcomes fall into disjoint categories, their counts add. PINs made of one, two or three letters have different lengths, so their categories do not overlap. With repetition allowed, the total is 26 + 26² + 26³ = 18,278. Multiplication counts the choices within each length; addition combines the alternatives.

“At least one” often suggests a complement

Among four-symbol PINs from 36 symbols, there are 36⁴ total. Those with no repeated symbol number 36 × 35 × 34 × 33. Thus PINs with at least one repeat number 1,679,616 − 1,413,720 = 265,896.

Use inclusion–exclusion for overlapping sets

There are 333 multiples of 3 and 200 multiples of 5 from 1 through 1,000. The 66 multiples of 15 belong to both sets, so adding 333 and 200 counts them twice. The number divisible by 3 or 5 is 333 + 200 − 66 = 467.

For three sets, add the three single-set counts, subtract the three pairwise intersections, then add back the triple intersection. The final addition restores elements that the pairwise corrections removed too many times.

“Or” includes the overlap

Multiples of 15 qualify because they meet both conditions. Inclusion–exclusion keeps each in the union once; it does not discard them.

5 · Select a group

Combinations ignore the order of selected members

An r-combination of n distinct objects is a subset of size r. From {1, 2, 3}, the pairs {1, 2} and {2, 1} are the same subset. Each chosen r-element subset can be ordered in r! ways, so dividing the ordered-selection count by r! gives:

C(n, r) = P(n, r)/r! = n!/[r!(n − r)!]   (0 ≤ r ≤ n)

Build a team with two types of members

From five boys and seven girls, a team with exactly three boys and two girls is formed by choosing three of five boys and two of seven girls. The choices are independent, so multiply: C(5, 3) × C(7, 2) = 10 × 21 = 210. Do not order the five teammates.

For a five-person team with at least one boy, subtract the all-girl teams from all teams: C(12, 5) − C(7, 5) = 792 − 21 = 771.

Ordered roles are not teams

President and secretary are different offices, so swapping them changes an assignment. A team is a set, so rearranging its list of members does not change the outcome.

6 · Identical objects collapse arrangements

Divide out swaps that nobody can distinguish

The eleven letters of MISSISSIPPI include four S's, four I's, two P's and one M. Starting with 11! labelled arrangements overcounts because swapping identical letters does not make a new visible word. Divide by the internal factorial for each repeated type:

11!/(4! × 4! × 2!) = 34,650

Choose positions instead

Choose four of eleven positions for the S's, four of the remaining seven for the I's, and two of the remaining three for the P's. The last position holds M. The product C(11, 4) × C(7, 4) × C(3, 2) gives the same 34,650 arrangements.

The general rule for n objects split into indistinguishable types of sizes n₁, n₂, … is n!/(n₁!n₂!⋯), provided those type sizes sum to n.

Why binomial coefficients appear in expansions

In (a + b)ⁿ, each of the n factors contributes either a or b. To get aⁿ⁻ᵏbᵏ, choose exactly k factors from which to take b. There are C(n, k) such choices, explaining the coefficient of that term.

Repeated letters differ from repeated selection

MISSISSIPPI uses a fixed multiset of letters and asks for its arrangements. The next topic chooses a multiset from categories where the supply of each category is unrestricted.

7 · Choose the same category again

Stars and bars count unordered selections with repetition

Select three items from categories {1, 2, 3, 4}, allowing a category to appear more than once. The outcomes {1, 1, 2} and {1, 2, 1} are the same selection because order does not matter. The lecture lists 20 such size-three multisets.

Encode the count in each category

Use three stars for the three selected items and three bars to separate four fixed categories. For example, x||x|x means one item from category 1, none from category 2, one from category 3 and one from category 4. The row has six positions; choosing which three hold stars gives C(6, 3) = 20.

In general, r selections from n categories use r stars and n − 1 bars. Choosing the star positions gives C(n + r − 1, r).

Repetition does not make order matter

The ordered count nʳ distinguishes sequences such as 1, 1, 2 and 2, 1, 1. Stars and bars counts them once as the same multiset. It assumes each category can be selected without a supply limit.

8 · Make the model explicit

Ask two questions before choosing a formula

First ask whether changing order makes a new outcome. Then ask whether an item or category may be selected again. The lecture's final table summarises the four basic cases for k choices from n possibilities:

Order matters, repetition allowed: nᵏ
Order matters, no repetition: P(n, k)
Order irrelevant, no repetition: C(n, k)
Order irrelevant, repetition allowed: C(n + k − 1, k)

A PIN has positions, so order matters. A team has no roles, so order does not matter. A word made from a fixed collection of repeated letters is a different setup from choosing categories with repetition; use the repeated-object arrangement formula for the former.

Extra restrictions still need care

The four formulas count unrestricted base cases. Restricted officer roles, a required type of team member, or overlapping categories need a careful construction, case split, complement or inclusion–exclusion before a formula applies.

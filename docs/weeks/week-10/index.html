Week 10 · Sequence

How do we describe a sequence and prove where its terms go?

Read indexed formulas, simplify finite sums and products, then turn the intuition of “getting close” into a rigorous limit proof.

By the end, you can…

  • read and write explicit formulas with the correct starting index;
  • expand, simplify, and reindex finite sums and products;
  • distinguish convergence from two different kinds of divergence;
  • choose a threshold for an ε–K limit proof;
  • apply limit laws with their required conditions.

1 · Know the starting index

A sequence gives a value at each index

The deck writes a sequence as a₀, a₁, a₂, … . With this index-zero convention, the even-number example is aₙ = 2n, giving 0, 2, 4, …, and the doubling example is aₙ = 2ⁿ, giving 1, 2, 4, … . An explicit formula computes a term directly from n. A recurrence instead uses earlier terms.

aₙ = 2n → 0, 2, 4, …   ·   aₙ = 2ⁿ → 1, 2, 4, …

Fibonacci has more than one description

The displayed Fibonacci sequence begins 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8. Its simple recurrence says each term after the first two equals the preceding two added together. The slide also gives an explicit formula involving (1 + √5)/2 and (1 − √5)/2. A closed formula exists, but the recurrence is usually easier for generating the next term.

“First term” does not always mean n = 1

Here a₀ is the first displayed term. Before using a formula or counting terms in a sum, check whether the index begins at zero or one.

2 · Read both endpoints

Summation notation and cancellation

The expression Σ from k = m to n of aₖ means aₘ + aₘ₊₁ + ⋯ + aₙ. Both bounds are included. Thus Σ from k = 0 to n of 2k starts with 0 and ends with 2n, and it has n + 1 terms.

Build or expand a sum

In the slide's first exercise, 1/n + 2/(n + 1) + ⋯ + (n + 1)/(2n) is Σ from i = 0 to n of (i + 1)/(n + i), for positive n. The endpoints confirm both the numerator and denominator patterns. In the next exercise, Σ from i = 0 to 2n of (−1)ⁱ/(i + 1) expands to 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − ⋯ − 1/(2n) + 1/(2n + 1). The final sign is positive because 2n is even.

1/[k(k + 1)] = 1/k − 1/(k + 1)
Σ from k = 1 to n of 1/[k(k + 1)] = 1 − 1/(n + 1) = n/(n + 1).

In that telescoping sum, every interior fraction cancels with its neighbor. Write the first and last few terms before crossing anything out; the answer consists of the surviving boundaries.

Do not lose the last term

A pattern in the middle does not determine the bounds. Substitute the lower and upper index into your proposed summand and check that they reproduce the first and final displayed terms.

3 · Difference or ratio?

Arithmetic and geometric sequences

An arithmetic sequence has constant difference d and formula aₙ = a₀ + nd. Its sum through index n has n + 1 terms, so pairing first and last gives (a₀ + aₙ)(n + 1)/2. A geometric sequence has constant ratio r and formula aₙ = a₀rⁿ.

Σ from k = 0 to n of a₀rᵏ = a₀(rⁿ⁺¹ − 1)/(r − 1)   when r ≠ 1.

When r = 1, the displayed fraction is undefined; the sum is simply (n + 1)a₀. State this special case instead of dividing by zero.

Exercise 8.1.5

The arithmetic list 2 + 5 + 8 + ⋯ + 302 has difference 3. There are (302 − 2)/3 + 1 = 101 terms, so its sum is 101(2 + 302)/2 = 15,352. The geometric list 2¹⁰ + 2¹¹ + ⋯ + 2²⁹ has 20 terms and sums to 2³⁰ − 2¹⁰.

Count terms before applying a formula

Index zero through n includes n + 1 terms, while exponent ten through twenty-nine includes twenty. A correct pattern with the wrong term count gives the wrong total.

4 · Multiplication and index changes

Products and reindexing

Capital pi denotes a product: Π from k = m to n of aₖ equals aₘaₘ₊₁⋯aₙ. In the lecture's example, Π from k = 1 to n of k/(k + 1) equals (1/2)(2/3)⋯[n/(n + 1)] = 1/(n + 1). The middle factors cancel, just as interior terms cancel in a telescoping sum.

Combine expressions over a shared range

When aₖ = k + 1 and bₖ = k − 1, the slide's Σaₖ + 2Σbₖ becomes Σ(aₖ + 2bₖ) = Σ(3k − 1), with the same bounds m through n. Likewise (Πaₖ)(Πbₖ) = Π(aₖbₖ) = Π(k² − 1). A constant moves into a sum once per term; a product follows its own multiplication rule.

Σ from k = 1 to n of 1/(k + 1) = Σ from j = 2 to n + 1 of 1/j,   where j = k + 1.

Change the bounds with the variable

Replacing k by j in the summand while keeping k's old bounds would add 1 and omit 1/(n + 1). Reindexing changes the labels and endpoints, never the underlying terms.

5 · Look at the whole tail

Which sequences approach a number?

The sequence 1/n approaches 0 as n grows; none of its positive terms needs to equal 0. Similarly n/(n + 1) = 1 − 1/(n + 1) approaches 1. The terms of 1 + (−1/2)ⁿ alternate around 1, but the alternating correction shrinks to zero, so this sequence also converges to 1.

Two ways to diverge

The deck plots xₙ = n + 1, which grows beyond every fixed number, and xₙ = (−1)ⁿ⁺¹, which keeps alternating between 1 and −1. Both lack a finite limit. The second remains bounded, showing that boundedness alone does not imply convergence.

Alternation is not automatically divergence

Compare (−1)ⁿ, whose jump never shrinks, with (−1/2)ⁿ, whose size tends to zero. A limit requires all sufficiently late terms to stay close to one target.

6 · Make “close” precise

The ε–K definition of a limit

For a real target L and ε > 0, the ε-neighborhood of L is the open interval (L − ε, L + ε). Saying x lies inside it is equivalent to |x − L| < ε. A sequence xₙ converges to L when every positive ε has a threshold K after which every term lies in that neighborhood.

∀ε > 0, ∃K = K(ε) such that ∀n ≥ K, |xₙ − L| < ε.

What the picture says

Early terms may lie outside the horizontal band. After the chosen K, all later terms must remain inside it. A smaller ε may require a larger K; that dependence is allowed. The claim is about the entire tail, not one conveniently chosen term.

Keep the quantifiers in order

Choose ε first, then find K. Showing that some large term is close to L is weaker than proving that every term from K onward is close.

7 · Choose a threshold

Prove convergence and divergence

A constant sequence xₙ = c is distance zero from c at every index. For xₙ = 1/n, choose an integer K > 1/ε. Then for every n ≥ K, |1/n − 0| = 1/n < ε. These simple proofs show the ε–K pattern.

A rational limit

For xₙ = (3n + 1)/(2n + 5), subtract the proposed limit 3/2:

|xₙ − 3/2| = 13/(4n + 10) < 13/(4n).

Given ε > 0, take an integer K > 13/(4ε). Then all n ≥ K satisfy |xₙ − 3/2| < ε.

Factorial growth beats 4ⁿ

Let aₙ = 4ⁿ/n!. For n ≥ 7, aₙ₊₁/aₙ = 4/(n + 1) ≤ 1/2. Thus aₙ ≤ a₇(1/2)ⁿ⁻⁷ for n ≥ 7. Given ε > 0, choose K large enough that this geometric bound is below ε. Then every later aₙ is below ε, proving aₙ → 0.

Persistent oscillation cannot fit one band

The terms of 1 + (−1)ⁿ are 0 and 2 at arbitrarily large odd and even indices. If a limit L existed, ε = 1/2 would eventually require both values to lie within 1/2 of L, which is impossible.

8 · Use the rules with care

Limit laws and their conditions

Limits pass through a sum, difference, product, and fixed positive integer power. They also pass through a quotient when the denominator terms are nonzero and their limit is nonzero. Check the component limits before using a rule.

Two calculations from the slides

Divide (2n³ + n²)/(n³ + 5) by n³ to obtain (2 + 1/n)/(1 + 5/n³), so the limit is 2. Also n/(1 + 2n) = 1/(1/n + 2) tends to 1/2; taking its fifth power gives 1/32.

An exponent-zero exception in the source slide

Slide 28 states c/nᵏ → 0 for k ∈ ℕ, while slide 4 includes zero in ℕ. The result needs k ≥ 1: at k = 0, c/n⁰ = c for every n, so its limit is c, not generally zero. The slide image is preserved, and this condition corrects its claim.

The quotient condition matters for the same reason: a denominator approaching zero cannot simply be replaced by its limit in a fraction.
